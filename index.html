How it works

Three things, every morning

1

Say it while it's still there

Dreams fade within minutes of waking. Open the Atlas in the dark, speak or type, and stop. No menus, no decisions, nothing to set up.

2

It builds what you said

The house with the red door. The woman who wouldn't speak. The road that sloped the wrong way. Only what you dreamt — the rest stays faint and unfinished, the way a half-remembered thing should.

3

Walk back in whenever you like

Your dreamscape keeps everything. Dream of that house again and it's still standing, with whatever you've since added to it. A year of dreams becomes a city with your own dead, your own gods and roads out to other worlds.

"I was in a tall house with arched windows and a red door. My grandmother was in the kitchen. Outside, a black dog followed me down a road that sloped the wrong way. Then I was under the sea."

Every one of those becomes something you can stand in front of.

The people stay themselves

A figure you dream twice is the same figure, in the same place, remembering what was said. They keep their own hours, their own homes and their own work.

The gods speak as themselves

Dream a deity by name and they come as their own tradition knows them — and in the manner you dreamt them: silent, stern, kind, riddling, grieving.

Nightmares can be faced

What chased you comes out after dark. You face it, and end it the way your dream ended it — or a way you choose, which then becomes true.

Why a map

Because memory is built for places

Dreams fade fast — most of a dream is gone within minutes of waking. But spatial memory is the strongest memory we have: it's why the old memory-palace technique works, and why trained volunteers using it roughly doubled what they could recall in a 2017 study published in Neuron.

The Atlas takes a dream and gives it back its geography. Instead of a page you never re-read, you get a street you can walk down.

Your dreams stay yours

They're kept on your own device. We can't read them — not the Temple, not anyone. That isn't a promise in a footer; it's how the thing is built.

It is not therapy

The Atlas isn't a medical device and makes no treatment claims. It's a journal that builds a world. If dreams are troubling you, talk to someone qualified.

Questions

The things people ask

Is this real, or a concept?

It's built and running. What the campaign funds is finishing it: the phone version, speaking your dream in the dark, and printing the book.

Do I have to write a lot?

No. A sentence builds something. The more you write, the more of the dream is there — but a line at four in the morning is enough.

What happens to my dreams?

They stay on your device. Nobody at the Temple can read them. Later there'll be an optional, anonymous research programme you can join or ignore — it's off unless you turn it on.

Is it a game?

No. There's nothing to win. It's a journal that builds a place, and you can walk in it.

Which platforms?

It runs in a browser today, on a computer or a phone. Proper phone apps are the first thing the campaign funds.

Is AI involved?

The Atlas reads your words and builds from them, and it works with no model at all. If you connect one, the figures you dreamt can talk back. Either way, nothing is invented that you didn't dream.

Do I pay now?

No. The list is free and nothing is charged. You'll get word the day the campaign opens, with the launch price.
